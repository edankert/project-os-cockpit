Nine ways to read the record

The cockpit has one shape — three panes and a document in the middle — and it is the right shape for reading a record and discharging what it owes. These are nine other shapes the same corpus supports. Each is a separate application with its own navigation, not a theme over the existing one; the matrix below is how that claim is checked.

1537 notes 16148 wikilinks 2206 distinct targets 12 repos in the fleet 70 notes owe a review response every figure measured 2026-09-05
Nothing here is accepted, and nothing is scheduled. No feature, task or phase is allocated against any of the nine. Two of them argue against decisions this project has already taken — ASK against PHASE-028's own out-of-scope list, DECK against ADR-0020 — and each says so on its own plate. The mocks are drawn from measured corpus figures, so what they show is this repo, not a plausible repo.

They differ where it counts

Six axes. Two designs sharing a row would be one design with two skins, so this table is the first thing to attack. The one that matters most is who decides the layout: an auto-laid graph, a fixed map, a queue that shows you one thing, and a table you arrange yourself are four genuinely different relationships with the same 1537 notes.

01 ORBIT the links, as a solid you fly through

16148 wikilinks over 1537 notes is a structure nobody in this project has ever seen. The cockpit resolves one link when you click it and lists backlinks at the foot of a note — the local view of a shape that has never been drawn whole. ORBIT draws the whole thing and gives you no list at all: you steer, and the corpus has an inside. Colour is the status band, straight off statuses.py, so the first thing the shape tells you is that this record is overwhelmingly finished work with a live crust on it.

Drag the field. Node colour is the status band; node size is inbound links; clusters are phases. Hovering an edge — not a node — surfaces the sentence in the source note that contains the wikilink, which is the one thing a graph view normally throws away.

Navigation

There is no tree, no list and no back button. You fly: drag to turn the field, scroll to close in, double-click to land on a note and open it. ⌘K flies the camera rather than jumping the view, so you keep your bearings — the note arrives from a direction.

Data it needs

Exists: wikilinks.py already resolves every link target, and the index holds every note's frontmatter. Status bands come from statuses.py, which is already the single vocabulary.

New: one endpoint returning the whole edge list, and the character offset of each link so the containing sentence can be pulled for the edge callout. Layout must be computed once and cached — a 16k-edge simulation cannot run on every open.

The objection

Graph views are famously beautiful and famously useless. Obsidian's is the standard example: people open it, say "wow", and never navigate with it. The honest question is whether ORBIT ever answers a question you actually have.

Its one candidate answer is structural: which notes nothing links to, and which clusters are joined by a single edge. That is a real query about this corpus — and it is also a query a table could answer without any of this.

02 ATLAS land you learn by heart

ORBIT moves and ATLAS never does. Every note has a permanent address, computed once and then frozen: FEAT-0143 is in the east, on the coast, next to the harbour where the fleet work landed, and it will be there next month and next year. That is the whole idea — a map you can remember, so finding something stops being a search and becomes a recollection. Regions are phases, and the map shows two things no list does: how much of the territory is old, drawn as fog, and where the record simply stops.

Drag to pan, scroll to zoom; the inset keeps the whole territory in view. Regions are this repo's real phases, settlements its real notes. The hatched band is the June silence — one note created in June against 180 in May and 313 in July — which is the kind of fact no note-scoped, phase-scoped or queue-scoped surface can show, because a silence belongs to none of them.

Navigation

Pan and zoom, exactly like a map application, plus the two things maps have that graphs do not: a permanent address and an inset that never loses the whole. You navigate by remembering where a thing was, not by searching for what it is called.

Places can be bookmarked as coordinates, so "the corner where the release work is" is a link you can send someone.

Data it needs

Exists: phases, every note's phase:, statuses, and updated: on every note — fog is just a date threshold over data the index already holds.

New: a layout pass that assigns coordinates and stores them, plus the rule for what happens when a note is added: it must take a plot in its region without moving its neighbours, or the map's whole promise breaks.

The objection

Frozen coordinates rot. A corpus that grew 634 notes in a single month will overflow whatever plots it was given, and the moment the map re-flows to fit, every address a person memorised is wrong — worse than never having promised one.

The fix is real work, not a detail: plots must be allocated with room to grow, and a region that fills up has to spill into new land at the edge rather than re-drawing the interior.

03 REEL the project as footage you can scrub

Every note carries created: and updated:, every change note is dated in its filename, and the snapshot has been committed on every change since January. That is enough to reconstruct what this project looked like on any past day — and no surface does it. REEL is a video editor's timeline over that: phases are tracks, work items are clips, and dragging the playhead rebuilds the left-hand panel as it stood at that instant. Data it needs

Exists: every date the mock uses is in frontmatter today, and git_state.py already reads the commit history the clips would be cut against.

New: a state-at-date query. Frontmatter records the current status, not when it changed, so the honest reconstruction has to come from git log over the notes — which is real work and the reason this is the most expensive of the nine.

The objection

Nobody asks what the project looked like in July. Time-travel views are built more often than they are used, and this one is the costliest to build honestly.

The counter is the span, not the instant: "what happened between these two dates" is asked at every close-out and every release, and today it is answered by reading commit messages. If REEL is built, it should be built for the selection, and the scrub is a side effect.

04 PULSE an instrument wall for work you are not doing

The agent-hook feed already carries everything an instrument needs: which agent, which repo, which files, which notes, the prompt, the cost, the context window's used_percentage, and whether the session has been editing code with no task note open. Data it needs

Exists, all of it. agent_hooks.py already records agent, session, prompts, files, docs_notes, cost, context percentage and the undocumented flag; the fleet already runs one sidecar per repo.

New: only the aggregation — one socket carrying every workspace's sessions at once, rather than each sidecar knowing about its own.

The objection

An instrument wall for one person running two agents is cosplay. Most of this screen is empty most of the time, and a dashboard that is usually idle trains you to stop looking at it — which is worse than not having it when the one real alarm fires.

The honest version of PULSE may be much smaller: the alarm row alone, delivered as a notification, with no wall at all.

05 ASK the record, answering

1537 notes is past the size where a person can find an answer by reading. Half the corpus is reasoning — why the ledger is JSON, why the sweep was withdrawn, why a verdict is an event — and that reasoning is exactly what a new session, or a returning human, needs and cannot locate. ASK puts a prompt in front of it: the answer is prose with citations, every claim traceable to the note it came from, and the two moves borrowed from t3.codes are forking a thread and switching the model mid-conversation so two readings of the same question sit side by side.

Data it needs

Exists: the full corpus, the index, and the wikilink graph for expanding a citation into its neighbours.

New: retrieval over the notes, a model call from the sidecar — which today has no outbound network path and deliberately so — and a rule for what an answer may claim. An answer that cites a note it did not read is exactly the failure DESIGN.md's first line forbids.

The objection

PHASE-028 already ruled this out, in writing. Its own out-of-scope list names "a conversation UI" among the things "T3 does well and the cockpit should not", on the ground that every hour spent building a harness is an hour not spent on the governance layer nobody else is building.

Proposing it anyway needs a reason that decision was wrong. The candidate: that phase was about driving agents, and this is about reading the record — a different thing that happens to share a text box. That may be a real distinction or a convenient one, and it is Edwin's call, not mine.

06 DECK one thing, then the next thing

Every other design here shows you more. DECK shows you less, on purpose: one obligation, filling the screen, with no way to see the queue behind it and no way to browse. You answer it or you defer it, and the next one comes up. The measured case for it is blunt — 70 notes in this repo carry a reviewer's changes-requested and 61 of them are already at a terminal status, a backlog that grew by 21 in the sixteen days since it was last written down. That is not a discovery problem. Nobody is going to browse their way out of it.

Click a verb, or use the arrow keys. The four cards are real obligations this repo owes right now — a stale verdict, an untriaged issue eleven days open, this design's own review, and three unpushed commits in a fleet repo.

Navigation

None, deliberately. No list, no search, no back. The queue picks; you judge. It is the Superhuman/Things model, and its whole value is removing the decision about what to look at, which is where a backlog of 61 actually dies.

Sized for a phone or a tablet on the sofa, because the state it is designed for is "ten minutes, not at my desk".

Data it needs

Exists: obligations.py already computes what is owed and approvals.py already knows which verbs are human-only. The stale-verdict rule already ships as REVIEW-STALE in the validator.

New: an ordering — the hard part. A queue that deals the wrong card first is worse than a list, because you cannot see past it to the one that mattered.

The objection

ADR-0020 says obligations live with their subject, not in a central queue you visit. DECK is precisely the central queue that decision rejected, and the reason it was rejected still holds: a verdict made without the note around it is a verdict made without its context.

The narrow defence is that a card is not a queue-with-a-list — it can carry the whole subject, and a card that cannot is one that should open the note instead. Whether that satisfies ADR-0020 or evades it is the argument to have before anyone builds this.

07 LEDGER the one with no pictures

Eight of these nine designs answer a question somebody anticipated. This one answers the question nobody did. Every note is a row, every frontmatter field is a column, and any combination of them is two clicks away — including combinations no view will ever be built for, like issues opened before August, still unowned, whose phase is closed. It is the escape hatch, it will be the least beautiful thing in the project, and it is the design most likely to be used every day.

Cells and keyboard. Arrow keys move, ⌘F filters, ⇧↓ extends a selection, ⌘⇧E exports. Views are saved and named, so a question you asked once becomes a place you can go back to — the closest thing here to a bookmark.

Data it needs

Exists: essentially all of it. The index parses every note's frontmatter already; this is a table over what is in memory.

New: the write path is the risk, not the read path. Bulk transition across 42 rows is 42 chances to corrupt the record, and every one of them has to pass the same server-side guards a single edit does.

The objection

A general tool answers every question badly. A table that can express anything expresses nothing in particular — which is why the cockpit's opinionated views exist, and why "add a filter for that" is the answer that has quietly killed a hundred good products.

There is also a real hazard: bulk edit makes it easy to set forty statuses at once, and a status set in bulk is a status nobody thought about.

08 BROADSHEET the week, set in type, on paper

The one design here that is not an application at all. Every Monday the corpus is typeset into a page: what shipped, what broke, what the record got wrong, and what died. It arrives by email, prints on A3, and reads on an e-ink tablet with no server running. Its real argument is the Corrections box — this project has now formally corrected its own documentation three times, and those corrections are currently buried in the files they correct. A newspaper is the only format where being wrong last week is a standing column rather than an embarrassment.

The failures were real and the tests were wrong, which is the harder of the two cases to spot. Each of the five asserted a distance between characters rather than the behaviour that distance happened to produce, so a legitimate change to the surface broke them without breaking anything a reader would notice.

The repair was not to make them pass. Four were rewritten to assert the observable behaviour; one was deleted, on the ground that nothing it covered was still true. The sweep then found twenty-one more of the same shape, which have been filed rather than fixed — the fix is a day's work and the filing is honest about that.

The wider lesson is recorded in the same change note: a suite that goes green after an edit tells you the edit did not break the suite, and nothing more. Where the suite is the only evidence, that is the whole verification argument, and it is thinner than it looks.

Also this week

The inbox takes any file type. Material dropped for triage is no longer restricted to text — a screenshot, an export or a log now lands intact, because the thing a person drops is rarely the thing a parser expects.

The writing rules reached the fleet. Every repo now carries the same prose guidance, after a reader reported the documentation was pitched "at a very high level of abstraction". The rules are unglamorous: point first, one idea per sentence, gloss your own vocabulary.

The gate is installed everywhere and blocks nowhere. Four repos migrated to the shared validator; acceptance checks now fire as warnings only, until the grandfather window closes on 20 November. Installing a gate and having it block are two different dates, and only one of them has happened.

Leader

“The cockpit is how a person governs a project they did not write. It must not be able to say something false about that project without saying so.”

That sentence is assembled from four notes and quoted in the release. It is also the standard this page is held to. A weekly paper generated from the record is the easiest place in the project to say something false — it summarises, and a summary is a claim with its evidence removed.

So every line above carries the note it came from, nothing on this page was written by hand, and the Corrections column runs whether or not there is anything cheerful to put beside it. A paper that only prints the good weeks is an advertisement.

09 FOUNDRY where the work is piling up

A project-os lifecycle is a production line — intake, planning, build, review, release — and every other view in this project shows you the items rather than the line. FOUNDRY shows the line: parcels travelling between stations, and a physical pile wherever they stop moving. Run it against this repo and the pile has one address. 61 finished notes are queued at Review, which is not a backlog you would ever see in a list sorted by anything.

The line runs; the pile grows. This is the one view where the bottleneck is a place rather than a number in a column, and where "we are shipping fine" and "we are shipping fine because nothing is being reviewed" look completely different.

Navigation

You watch first, then click a station to open its queue. Dragging a parcel to another station is a status transition — the gesture and the write are the same act, which is either the most direct interface here or the most dangerous.

Zoom goes to a station, never to an item; an individual note is a detail you leave the floor to read.

Data it needs

Exists: statuses map onto stations directly, and obligations.py already knows what is stuck and why.

New: arrival and departure times per station — which is the same missing history REEL needs. Without it there is no cycle time, no flow rate, and the floor is just a pretty status chart.

The objection

A factory is the wrong metaphor for judgment. The Review station is "slow" because a human is deciding, and a screen that renders deliberation as a jam quietly argues that the fix is to clear the pile faster.

This project's whole thesis is that some steps must be a person. A view that makes those steps look like inefficiency is not neutral, and it would need to say so on its face.

Seven more, considered and not drawn

Each was a candidate for one of the nine slots. The reason it lost is more useful than the concept.

Sonification

The repository as a score — a tone per commit, a chord per phase, dissonance when the validator fails. Cut because sound carries about one bit and cannot be re-read. It is a notification channel wearing an interface's clothes.

The ambient garden

A second screen where the project grows or wilts with its health. Cut on this project's own rule: a decorative surface cannot say "unknown", and DESIGN.md is explicit that unknown and fine are different sentences. A wilting plant is also a guilt machine, which is not governance.

The detective board

A freeform canvas where you pin notes and draw the string — the only layout model none of the nine uses, since every one of them decides the arrangement for you. Cut narrowly, and it is the strongest of the seven. The doubt: the corpus already has an authored link graph, and hand-drawn string would quietly compete with related:.

Room-scale / VR

Walk through the corpus at body scale. Cut because the hardware is not on the desk, and 3D already appears twice in the nine. A headset view whose advantage over ORBIT is "bigger" is not a different application.

The pager

Push notification, one-tap approve, nothing else. Cut because FEAT-0079 already owns supervision from a phone. Drawing it here would restate a planned feature as though it were a new idea.

The effort Sankey

Ribbons showing where the work actually went, phase by phase. Cut on data: nothing in this corpus records effort. The ribbons would be item counts drawn as though they were hours, which is a chart that lies about its own units.

The blame reader

A note rendered with every line tinted by when — and by which session — it was written. Cut because it is a feature of the reader that exists, not an application beside it. It belongs in the cockpit's document pane, and it is a good idea there.

If anything gets built

Nine proposals is not a plan, and picking is yours. This is the order I would argue for, and the reasoning is about cost and about what each one would teach, not about which is most interesting to draw.

  1. LEDGER, because it is nearly free and it measures demand for the other eight

    Every field it needs is already parsed and in memory; it is a table over the index. Build it read-only — no bulk edit, none of the risk — and then watch which saved views get used. A view somebody re-runs weekly is a purpose-built surface waiting to be designed, and that is a far better way to choose among the other eight than my opinion or yours.

  2. REEL's selection, without REEL's scrub

    "What happened between these two dates" is asked at every close-out and every release, and today it is answered by reading commit messages. That half needs the git-derived history the expensive half also needs — so build the query, answer the real question with it, and only draw the timeline if the query turns out to be worth looking at.

  3. Then one exploratory one, chosen for the argument it settles

    ORBIT and ATLAS answer the same underlying question — what is the shape of 16148 links — and disagree about whether motion or permanence is the way to show it. Building either one settles that. Building both would be building the same thing twice, and the matrix at the top is where you check that claim.

  4. And two of them need a decision before a line is written

    ASK contradicts PHASE-028's out-of-scope list and DECK contradicts ADR-0020. Neither should be prototyped "to see how it feels" — that is how a recorded decision gets overturned by a demo rather than by an argument. If either is worth building, the ADR changes first.
